# 10 — Glossary

Every term the app might show a user, in plain English. This is a **product surface**, not just documentation: it's the source content for the `JargonChip` component ([06](06-design-system.md#zonechip--disciplinechip--jargonchip)), so the tone here is the tone the user reads.

Rules for writing an entry:
- Lead with what it *is* in one sentence, no jargon inside the definition.
- Then why the athlete should care.
- Never assume prior knowledge. Never be condescending.
- Under 40 words where possible.

---

## The race

**Triathlon** — Swim, then bike, then run, back to back, timed as one continuous event. The clock never stops, including while you're changing.

**Super sprint** — About 400 m swim, 10 km bike, 2.5 km run. The gentlest way in. Often an hour or less.

**Sprint** — 750 m swim, 20 km bike, 5 km run. The most common first triathlon. Most first-timers finish in 1½–2 hours.

**Olympic (or Standard)** — 1.5 km swim, 40 km bike, 10 km run. Roughly double a sprint. Typically 2½–3½ hours.

**Half / 70.3** — 1.9 km swim, 90 km bike, 21.1 km run. "70.3" is the total distance in miles. A serious undertaking; usually 5–8 hours.

**Full / Ironman / 140.6** — 3.8 km swim, 180 km bike, 42.2 km run. A full marathon after all that. 10–17 hours.

**Duathlon** — Run, bike, run. No swimming. A genuinely good option if the swim is the blocker — it is a real race, not a consolation prize.

**Aquabike** — Swim and bike only. Useful if you're injured and can't run.

**Wave** — Athletes start in groups rather than all at once, usually by age or ability. Your time starts when *your* wave starts.

**Rolling start** — Instead of a mass start, athletes enter the water a few at a time. Far less intimidating, and increasingly common.

**Cut-off** — The time limit for a section or the whole race. Most beginner-friendly races have generous ones. Worth checking, then forgetting about.

**DNF** — Did Not Finish. It happens to everyone eventually, including professionals. It is not a moral failing.

**DNS** — Did Not Start.

**AG** — Age Group. Non-professional athletes, ranked within their age band. That's almost everyone.

## Transitions

**Transition** — The area where your bike and gear are racked, and where you change between disciplines. Your time keeps running.

**T1** — Swim to bike. Wetsuit off, helmet on, shoes on, out you go.

**T2** — Bike to run. Bike racked, helmet off, running shoes on.

**Rack** — The metal bar your bike hangs on, and the verb for putting it there.

**Body marking** — Race staff write your number on your arm and leg in marker. Normal. Comes off in a day or two.

**Mount / dismount line** — The line where you're allowed to get on and off your bike. Getting on before it is a penalty. Walking your bike to it is completely fine.

**Flying mount** — Jumping onto a moving bike with the shoes already clipped in. Looks impressive. Absolutely not necessary. Ignore it for now.

## The swim

**Open water** — Any swim that isn't in a pool: lake, river, sea. No lane ropes, no walls, no black line. Different skill, and it takes practice.

**Sighting** — Lifting your eyes forward every few strokes to check you're swimming in the right direction. Without it people swim impressive distances sideways.

**Wetsuit legal** — Whether wetsuits are allowed, decided by water temperature on the day. Wetsuits make you warmer *and* faster because they float you.

**Cold shock** — The gasp-and-panic reaction when you first get into cold water. It passes in 60–90 seconds. Knowing it's coming is most of the battle: get in slowly, put your face in, breathe out.

**Tow float** — An inflatable bag you tow behind you on a belt. Makes you visible, gives you something to hold. Use one every time you swim outdoors.

**Drafting (swim)** — Swimming just behind someone to save effort. Legal in the swim, and worth learning.

**CSS (Critical Swim Speed)** — Roughly the fastest pace you can hold in the water for a long time. We work it out from a 400 m and a 200 m time trial and use it to set your swim paces.

**Catch** — The part of the stroke where your hand grabs the water. Where most of your speed comes from.

**Bilateral breathing** — Breathing on both sides, usually every 3 strokes. Keeps you swimming straight and gives you options in choppy water.

## The bike

**Brick** — A bike session followed immediately by a run. It teaches your legs to cope with the weird jelly feeling of running off the bike — which is the single strangest sensation in triathlon, and much less alarming once you've felt it in training.

**Cadence** — How fast you're pedalling, in revolutions per minute. Most people are comfortable around 80–90.

**Drafting (bike)** — Riding close behind someone to save effort. **Illegal in most age-group triathlons.** Leave the required gap or you'll get a penalty.

**Clipless pedals** — Confusingly, these are the pedals you *do* clip into with special shoes. Optional. Flat pedals and trainers are completely fine for your first race.

**Aero bars** — Handlebar extensions that let you tuck into a lower position. Faster, but they need practice before you use them in a crowd.

**Turbo trainer** — A device that turns your normal bike into a stationary bike indoors. The single best way to train consistently in bad weather or the dark.

**FTP (Functional Threshold Power)** — Roughly the power in watts you can hold for an hour. Requires a power meter. Entirely optional — you can train perfectly well without one.

**Watts** — A direct measure of how hard you're pedalling, unaffected by wind or hills. Which is why people like it.

---

## The run

**Run off the bike** — Running immediately after cycling. Your legs feel strange for the first 5–10 minutes. This is normal and it passes.

**Negative split** — Running the second half faster than the first. The polite way to pace, and rarer than it should be.

**Cadence (run)** — Steps per minute. Around 170–180 suits many people, but there's no magic number.

**Walk break** — Walking on purpose, as strategy. Completely legal, used by plenty of finishers, and often faster overall than blowing up. Not cheating.

## Training

**Zone** — A band of effort, from very easy (Zone 1) to flat out (Zone 5). Most of your training should be in Zones 1–2, which usually feels *too easy*. It isn't.

**RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion)** — How hard it felt, 1 to 10. Unfashionably simple and surprisingly reliable.

**Talk test** — Judging effort by what you can say. Full conversation = easy. Short sentences = steady. A few words = hard. One word = very hard.

**Threshold** — The hardest effort you could hold for about an hour. The reference point most training zones are built from.

**LTHR (Lactate Threshold Heart Rate)** — Your heart rate at threshold. Different for running and cycling — usually higher for running — so they're tested separately.

**Max HR** — The highest your heart rate goes. Age formulas estimate it badly; if we haven't measured yours, we say so.

**Aerobic base** — The endurance foundation built by lots of easy training. Boring to build, and everything else sits on top of it.

**Periodization** — Structuring training into phases that build on each other, rather than doing the same thing every week.

**Base / Build / Peak / Taper** — The phases. Base builds endurance, Build adds race-specific intensity, Peak sharpens, Taper rests you so you arrive fresh.

**Taper** — The last 1–3 weeks before a race, where training volume drops sharply. It feels wrong. You'll feel twitchy and convinced you're losing fitness. You aren't — this is when the training you've already done finally surfaces.

**Recovery week** — A deliberately easy week, usually every 3rd or 4th. Not optional, and not a week off.

**Ramp rate** — How fast your training load increases week to week. Too fast is the single most common route to injury, which is why we cap it.

**Load** — One number summarizing how much a session cost you, combining how long and how hard. An hour at threshold = 100.

**Fitness / Fatigue / Freshness** — Fitness is your training built up over about 6 weeks. Fatigue is the tiredness from the last week or so. Freshness is the difference. You want fitness high and fatigue low on race day — which is exactly what the taper is for.

**Overtraining** — Training more than you can recover from. Signs: everything feels hard, sleep is bad, resting heart rate is up, motivation is gone. The fix is rest, not effort.

**Cross-training** — Any other activity that builds fitness. Useful when injured or bored.

**Time trial (TT)** — Going as hard as you can for a set distance or time, usually to test where your fitness is.

**Interval** — A hard effort followed by a recovery period, repeated.

**Tempo** — Comfortably hard. Sustainable for a while, but you wouldn't want to chat.

**Long run / long ride** — The week's longest session, done easy. Builds endurance and mental durability.

## Kit

**Tri suit** — A one-piece you swim, bike and run in without changing. The single most useful purchase for a first race.

**Trisuit chamois** — The thin pad in a tri suit. Thinner than a cycling one because you have to run in it.

**Race belt** — An elastic belt for your race number, so you can spin it from back (bike) to front (run) without pinning anything.

**Bento box** — A small bag on the bike top tube for food.

**Bottle cage** — Holds your drink bottle. Practise grabbing and replacing it while moving before race day.

**Gels** — Concentrated carbohydrate in a sachet. Test them in training. Never try a new one on race day.

**Bonk / hitting the wall** — Running out of available fuel. Sudden, unmistakable, and preventable by eating regularly.

---

## Fuelling

**Carbs per hour** — Roughly how much carbohydrate to eat per hour of racing. Sprints need almost nothing; longer races need a plan.

**Electrolytes** — Salts lost in sweat. Matter over long or hot races.

**Nothing new on race day** — The oldest rule in endurance sport. Every gel, drink, sock and strap should have been tested in training.

---

## App terms

**Load Units (LU)** — Our measure of how much a session cost. One hour at threshold = 100 LU.

**Plan phase** — Which stage of training you're in: Prep, Base, Build, Peak, Taper.

**Adaptation** — When the app changes your upcoming plan based on what actually happened. It always tells you what it changed and why, and you can always undo it.

**Key session** — A session that matters more than the others that week. If you only do one, do this one.

**Race simulation** — A training session that rehearses race day: same kit, same fuelling, similar effort, shorter distance.

**Readiness** — An optional daily score from sleep, soreness and motivation, used to soften or sharpen today's session. Advice, never an instruction.
